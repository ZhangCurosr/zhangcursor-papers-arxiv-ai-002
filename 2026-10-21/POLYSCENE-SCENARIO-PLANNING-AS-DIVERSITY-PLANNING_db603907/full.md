## ABSTRACT

Scenario planning is a foresight practice in which analysts construct a small set of plausible futures and prepare an organisation for each. Each scenario is a narrative that must be plausible, internally consistent, relevant and challenging, and the set as a whole must be differentiated. The practice remains manual: a workshop (i.e., a facilitated meeting in which a small group of experts selects the drivers and writes the narratives) considers only the factors it has time for, carries the biases of the experts who run it, and cannot be redone whenever conditions change. In this paper, we automate scenario planning by casting it as a diversity planning problem, in which two scenarios differ when they end in different narratives on the analyst’s drivers. The result is a set of scenarios, one for each narrative that the topic can reach within a budget (i.e., a bound on a narrative’s cost). Our implementation, Polyscene, is a generic engine that generates scenarios from a topic (i.e., a planning domain model of the subject. We use geopolitical conflicts and shipping-route disruptions as case studies. For the first, we build the planning problem’s initial state from the GDELT Project so it reflects current relationships among the countries involved. Polyscene then generates scenarios showing how relationships among those countries change. With a board of six countries as of 1 January 2021 and a budget of five events, the conflict set puts five of the 15 country pairs at war, and each of the three wars that break out among these countries by 1 January 2026 ends a scenario. For the second, we build the initial state from ship traffic, a supply-pressure index, and the news, and Polyscene generates scenarios showing what a closed or diverted route could do to a manufacturer’s parts stock. With boards of four routes as of the same date and the same budget, each set holds three outcomes (e.g., the route closed, the lines diverted, or both), and the Suez Canal set holds the blockage of March 2021 and the diversion of container lines from the Red Sea from December 2023.

## 1 Introduction

Scenario planning is a foresight method in which analysts prepare for the future without trying to forecast it [1, 2]. They develop a limited set of credible alternative futures, called scenarios, and the organisation prepares to respond to each one. Each scenario is a narrative explaining how current conditions might unfold into a particular future. The approach gained prominence at Shell in the early 1970s. Its planning group set a family of scenarios ranging from producer states restricting oil supply to one in which no crisis occurred and growth continued [1]. When the 1973 embargo occurred, Shell’s leadership had already worked through the disruption storyline [1, 3]. Today, analysts typically convene in workshops (i.e., facilitated meetings in which a small group of experts selects the drivers and writes the narratives) to identify key drivers of change, craft a small set of narratives, and assess them against the discipline’s criteria [4, 5]. This approach has three shortcomings that argue for automation. First, a workshop considers only a limited number of factors and produces three to five narratives [4], so some futures are never explored. Second, it is vulnerable to expert bias. Scenario planning is meant to counter biases such as tunnel vision and anchoring, and the evidence that it does so is mixed [5], so a workshop can still fall prey to them. Tunnel vision directs attention towards futures that feel most familiar, while anchoring pulls each narrative towards the initial assumption encountered. Third, the exercise is infeasible to redo each time conditions change [6, 7]. These shortcomings leave structured scenario planning largely limited to organisations that can support dedicated foresight teams, while smaller organisations and public-sector bodies conduct it infrequently [8]. Our approach addresses these limitations in three ways. First, it can account for more factors than a workshop. A workshop usually builds its stories around a small set of drivers, often focusing on the two considered most uncertain, then combining them to produce four scenario themes [2]. In one conflict topic case study, it aims to predict future geopolitical conflicts. The topic models conflict as pairs between countries, so the space of possible narratives is $2 ^ { \mathrm { i 5 } }$ war combinations rather than just four themes. The engine then explores all narratives allowed by the topic’s events within the budget and produces one scenario for every combination it can reach. Second, our approach does not rank narratives by cost, likelihood, or other soft factors, so familiar futures have no search advantage over unfamiliar ones. Third, the analyst can rerun it whenever conditions change, and the same topic then returns a new set. Previous work has automated certain aspects of scenario planning. What remains missing is a trajectory-level criterion that defines when a generated chain of events counts as a scenario. For example, the IBM Scenario Planning Advisor (SPA) scores each generated plan using a cost derived from the estimated likelihoods, then clusters these plans into scenarios [9, 10, 11, 12]. Another line of work, such as cross-impact balance and morphological analysis, finds driver combinations that satisfy a consistency metric [13, 14]. Although these methods state what makes a driver combination acceptable, they return an end state rather than a sequence of events. More recent studies use large language models to draft foresight scenarios [15] and, in other fields, to generate test scenarios for autonomous driving [16]. While these narratives read smoothly, they often fail to remain consistent [17], and hallucinations weaken their credibility [18].

Our approach frames scenario planning as diversity planning, with one change. Instead of specifying a single goal state, we impose a budget on the plan’s cost. Any state reachable within that limit becomes a possible future, and any plan counts as a narrative. Not every narrative is a scenario (i.e., plausible, internally consistent, relevant, and challenging), so we encode the scenario criteria into the planning problem (Section 3). The workflow starts with developing the planning domain (i.e., the fluents and actions of the subject). The organisation’s question then turns the domain into a topic (i.e., the domain together with the actors’ objectives, constraints, concerns, and drivers that the criteria read). A script grounds the topic’s initial state from the record, and our engine then returns one scenario for each narrative that the topic can reach within the budget. We implement our approach, Polyscene, in Answer Set Programming [19]<sup>1</sup>. The engine is generic because the criteria are stated over the structure that every planning problem has (i.e., who acts on whom, what an action reads and changes, and what the actors want). This means that they can be implemented once and the topic supplies the planning domain. In summary, the main contributions of this paper are: (1) a formulation of scenario planning as a diversity planning problem, (2) a generic engine, called Polyscene, that generates one scenario per narrative a given topic can reach within the budget (Sections 3.1.5 and 3.2), and (3) two case studies, on geopolitical conflicts and on supply chains (Section 4). As of 1 January 2021, the conflict set includes 8 narratives within a budget of 5 events, and each of the three wars that broke out among these countries by 1 January 2026 ends a scenario. On four shipping-route boards as of the same date, each supply-chain set includes three outcomes (e.g., the route closed, the lines diverted, or both). The Suez Canal set holds the blockage of the canal in March 2021 and the diversion of container lines from the Red Sea from December 2023, and it misses the production stops of January 2024. Section 2 reviews scenario planning and automated planning, using Shell’s exercise as a running example; Section 5 compares our approach with prior work on automating scenario planning; and Section 6 discusses the findings and suggests future work.

## 2.1 Scenario Planning

A scenario planning exercise begins by identifying the factors that influence a given topic’s future. A subset of these factors is then selected as scenario drivers (i.e., the factors whose values distinguish one future from another) [5]. Each scenario is then developed into a narrative describing how the present situation could evolve. Kahn and Wiener [20] define a scenario as a hypothetical sequence of events constructed to focus attention on causal processes and decision points. Analysts evaluate scenarios by several criteria, including plausibility, internal consistency, relevance and challenge [4, 3]. A fifth criterion applies to the set rather than a single scenario: the scenarios must be differentiated [4].

A scenario is plausible if it can happen [21]. This does not imply it must be likely [22, 23]. Plausibility does not rank scenarios, so we view all scenarios within a set as equally plausible [4, 5]. A scenario is internally consistent when the logics it combines are consistent [21]. A scenario is relevant when it offers specific insights into the future that bear on the decision at hand [21]. This means that relevance depends on the audience, since it is judged against the concerns of the organisation for which the scenario is written [3, 2]. A scenario is considered challenging if it challenges the organisation’s conventional expectation about the future [21], which van der Heijden [3] describe as presenting a new viewpoint on the matters at hand. Finally, a collection of scenarios is differentiated when its scenarios differ structurally rather than as variations on one base case [21]. To clarify these requirements, we use Shell’s 1972 exercise as our running example throughout this paper. Note that Example 1’s four outcomes represent final configurations, each compatible with numerous distinct trajectories. An analyst cannot write out all these trajectories by hand, motivating automation. Section 5 reviews earlier efforts to automate parts of this exercise.

Example 1 (Shell’s exercise). We use a simplified version of Shell’s exercise, set in 1972 before the October 1973 Egyptian–Israeli war broke out. The actors and events below are ours and follow the 1973 embargo; Shell’s own scenarios focused on producer revenues and oil supply rather than war [1]. The key participants include the producer cartel (OPEC), an oil-importing country that supports a regional ally (the United States), the ally itself (Israel), and a neighbouring state that is hostile to the ally (Egypt). The United States is allied with Israel, while Israel and Egypt are adversaries. The cartel operates as a unified, coordinated entity. Each participant has a certain capacity to endure economic harm. For instance, the cartel relies on oil revenues, while the importer depends on its ability to finance oil purchases. Two elements of this situation are identified as scenario drivers: (i) whether the cartel maintains coordinated behaviour, and (ii) whether the region remains peaceful or descends into war. These drivers produce four possible outcomes, and we examine two, as Shell’s team examined two families of futures. In the disruption narrative, war breaks out, the importing country resupplies its ally, and the still-coordinated cartel responds with an embargo that severely restricts the importer’s capacity. In the continuity narrative, coordination among cartel members deteriorates, the region remains peaceful, and economic growth continues. We leave the remaining two outcomes unexplored, as an analyst would consider them less insightful. One of these unexamined outcomes features a united cartel in a peaceful region, which Shell’s surprise-free scenario (i.e., the future in which nothing disturbs the present trends) took as the projection at the time [1].

## 2.2 Narrative Planning

Section 2.1 outlined scenario planning and the criteria any scenario must fulfill. This subsection defines the planning problem the engine builds on. Automated planning studies how to find a sequence of actions that takes the world from an initial state to one that satisfies a goal [24]. Our setting differs from this in two ways. We set the goal to $G = { \mathsf { T } } \left( { \mathsf { i . e . } } \right.$ every final state satisfies it) and instead bound a narrative’s cost, because a scenario exercise has no single goal state, as it explores a potential future. Our setting also needs to rule out entire sequences of actions, which preconditions cannot do, because a precondition is checked only in the state where its action occurs. In Shell’s exercise, the cartel may split after it imposes an embargo, since it is coordinated at both moments, yet the embargo stays in force once the cartel splits, and an embargo enforced by a divided cartel is contradictory. Adding a negated embargo to the precondition of the split would have to be repeated for every action that could divide the cartel, whereas a trajectory constraint (i.e., a formula over the whole sequence of states and actions) states the invariant once (i.e., an embargo in force requires a coordinated cartel) and judges every state the sequence passes through. Admitting actions as atoms extends beyond PDDL3, which focuses on state constraints, and aligns with LTL action theories, where an action is a proposition related to its resulting situation [25]. This approach combines PDDL3’s state constraints with the action-trajectory constraints Bonassi et al. [26] added to PDDL3, which range over actions alone. Thus, we model these invariants and scenario criteria as constraints and preferences in PDDL3 [27], forcing the planner to consider the states it passes through to satisfy them. Following Wright et al. [28], De Giacomo and Vardi [29], we express the planning problem and its solution as follows.

In scenario planning, we pair the planning problem Ξ with the inputs that the scenario criteria read, and we call the pair a topic T, which takes the form of the inputs to a narrative planning problem [31]. Narrative planning plans a story for an author: a planning problem whose actions are performed by characters, each with its own goals, and whose plans must reach the author’s goal while each character acts in its own interest. Ware and Siler [32] assigns each character a numeric utility instead of goals. We use the same inputs, with the actors as the characters and their objectives as the utilities, and we replace the author’s goal with the scenario criteria. The analyst writes these utilities using another PDDL3 feature, preference (i.e., a soft constraint a plan may violate at a price): a preference names a condition on the final state, and the problem’s metric charges each violated preference a weight. We give each actor its own metric, so an actor’s objective is the weighted sum of the preferences it holds at a state, and an action serves the actor when it raises that sum. Section 3.1.4 defines the objective and states how the engine reads it. PDDL3 allows one metric per problem, so the preferences appear in the problem file, with the actors’ metrics beside them, as Listing 1 shows. In this work, every action costs 1, so a narrative’s cost is its number of actions; we leave actions with different costs for future work. The budget never ranks one narrative above another. ## 2.3 Planners

In Section 2.2, we introduced a planning problem whose plans are narratives. Since a scenario exercise requires multiple narratives, this subsection defines the problem of finding a set of plans and reviews the planners that solve it. Abdelwahed [33] states this problem generically, using a diversity indicatorfunction (i.e., a function that maps a set of plans to a number, with larger values indicating greater diversity).

Definition 4 (Diversity planning problem). Given a planning problem $\Xi ,$ a cost bound $c ,$ a number of plans $k ,$ and a diversity indicator function $\lambda : \bar { 2 } ^ { \Pi \Xi } \to \breve { . }$ R over sets of plans, a diversity planning problem is to find a set of plans $\Psi \Xi \subseteq \Pi _ { \Xi }$ such that cos $\operatorname { \Pi } ( \pi ) \leq c$ for all $\pi \in \Psi { \Xi } \operatorname { a n d } | \Psi { \Xi } | \leq k$ , and that either maximises $\lambda ( \Psi \Xi )$ or satisfies $\lambda ( \Psi \Xi ) \geq d$ for a given threshold d.

A solution to a diversity planning problem is a plan set containing at most k plans for $\Xi ,$ each within the cost bound, and is either optimal in indicator value or has value at least d. Diverse planners solve this problem. A diverse planner generates a set of distinct plans according to a diversity model, often a distance function that measures plan similarity. Early methods relied on domain-specific knowledge: Tate et al. [34] with O-Plan, and Myers and Lee [35] with an HTN planner guided by metatheoretic knowledge. The first domain-independent method was by Srivastava et al. [36], who modified the constraint-based planner GP-CSP and the local-search planner LPG to produce plans with pairwise distances above a threshold. Other planners varied the distance metric or search strategy, such as DLAMA [37], which uses landmarks, and LPG-d [38], which requires a minimum action distance. Katz and Sohrabi [39] approached the planning task differently, developing the FI based on Vadlamudi and Kambhampati [40]’s two-phase method. The first phase generates a large plan set, and the second selects a diverse subset using clustering or greedy selection. Katz et al. [41] use mixed-integer programming to find an optimally diverse set, while Zhong et al. [42] use beam search based on quality and diversity. Abdelwahed [33] introduced the FBI, which considers diversity during planning while respecting the cost bound, by forbidding the behaviour of every plan it finds.

Narrative planners use the same inputs as a topic, but none of them is diverse planners, so we cannot use them to generate scenarios. Besides, narrative planners expect a goal state, whereas scenario planning asks which final states can arise. Examples of narrative planners include: Riedl and Young [31] search the space of partial plans for a single story in which characters act intentionally; Haslum [43] reformulates the problem as classical planning, so a classical planner returns one story per call; Porteous and Cavazza [44] steer a planner toward one story by constraining its trajectory; and Sabre [32] searches for one story in which every character acts on its own utility.

## 3 Plyscene: A Generic Scenario Planning Engine

Section 2.2 defined a planning problem whose plans are topic narratives, and Section 2.3 defined the diversity planning problem and the planners that solve it. This section presents Polyscene, the engine that generates scenarios for any given topic. The engine takes a topic T and a budget c and returns one scenario for each driver outcome that a scenario within budget can reach, so the analyst reads one narrative per future rather than a pile of variations on the same future. The engine is designed to be generic as follows. The five criteria are formulated and implemented once for every planning problem structure. This structure includes an action’s actor and target, the fluents that define its preconditions, effects, objectives, and drivers. The engine does not change its rules based on the specific details of a topic. Instead, a new topic only changes the facts the engine interprets.

Section 2.1 gave the criteria that the engine enforces. These criteria are (1) a scenario is plausible when it can happen; (2) internally consistent when the logics it combines agree; (3) relevant when it bears on the organisation’s concerns; (4) challenging when it contests the organisation’s conventional expectation; and (5) differentiation enforces that the scenarios must differ structurally rather than as variations on one base case. Each criterion that judges one scenario becomes a trajectory constraint (Definition 1) that the engine adds to Φ, so the extended planning problem’s narratives are exactly the scenarios. Differentiation compares narratives, so it decides which narratives the engine returns rather than which narratives exist. We define a scenario as follows.

Definition 5 (Scenario). Given a topic T with planning problem Ξ, a scenario is a plausible, internally consistent, relevant, and challenging narrative of $\Xi$ (Definitions 6 to 9). We denote the set of all scenarios of the topic as $\Pi _ { \Xi } ^ { s c } \subseteq \Pi _ { \Xi }$

Section 3.1 formulates each criterion as a trajectory constraint, uses Shell’s exercise as a running example, and defines the scenario-planning problem. Section 3.2 gives the planning-as-satisfiability query that carries the constraints, and Section 3.3 implements each constraint in Answer Set Programming (ASP) [19].

## 3.1 The Criteria as Trajectory Constraints

Each subsection covers a criterion regarding the scenario planning literature, applies it to Shell’s exercise, defines it, and provides its trajectory constraint $\varphi _ { \mathbf { x } } .$

## 3.1.1 Internal Consistency

A scenario is internally consistent when it contains no inherent contradictions or inconsistencies [21]. Schoemaker [2] evaluates this consistency by asking whether the scenario’s different outcomes can realistically occur together. Which values can occur together is a question about the subject, so the analyst answers it when writing $\Xi ,$ with two patterns that Listing 1 shows. The first is an invariant $( \mathrm { i . e . , a }$ formula $\psi$ over V that must hold in every state), which the analyst writes as the trajectory constraint □ψ in Φ (e.g., an embargo in force requires a coordinated cartel, so □(embargoed → coordinated)). The second is a fluent that records that an action has happened and whose precondition requires it to be false, so the action does not repeat. For instance, the embargo sets embargoed and requires it to be false, so the cartel imposes no second embargo, much as a country does not mobilise against the same country twice. The second pattern needs no constraint, since a plan satisfies every precondition, so the criterion reduces to the invariants. We define internal consistency as follows.

Definition 6 (Internal consistency). Given a topic T with planning problem Ξ and the invariants $\psi _ { 1 } , \ldots , \psi _ { l }$ of the subject (i.e., the formulas over V whose constraints $\sqcup \psi _ { 1 } , \ldots , \sqcup \psi _ { i }$ the analyst wrote into Φ), a narrative $\pi = \langle a _ { 1 } , \ldots , a _ { m } \rangle$ with the states $s _ { 0 } = I$ and $s _ { i } = \gamma ( s _ { i - 1 } , a _ { i } )$ is internally consistent if every state s satisfies every invariant $\psi _ { 1 } , \ldots , \psi _ { l }$

The constraint $\varphi _ { \mathrm { c o n s } } = \textstyle \bigwedge _ { k = 1 } ^ { l } \bigsqcup \psi _ { k }$ implements Definition 6 in $\operatorname { L T L } _ { f }$ . In Shell’s exercise, $\varphi _ { \mathrm { c o n s } } = \square ( e m b a r g o e d $ coordinated) rejects ⟨outbreak, resupply, embargo, split⟩, whose final state has the embargo in force and the cartel divided.

## 3.1.2 Relevance

According to Wilson [21], a scenario is relevant when it offers specific insights into the future that aid in decisionmaking. We capture this through the topic’s concern fluents C (Definition 3), because a scenario where concerns remain unchanged offers little insight for decision-making. We use a concern’s final value as a stand-in for insight because decision-makers tend to interpret a scenario by how it ends. In Shell’s exercise, the concern is the importer’s capacity (i.e., $C = \{ c a p a c i t y \} )$ . The disruption narrative $\pi _ { d } = \langle o u t b r e a k$ , resupply, embargo⟩ concludes with capacity at low, as the embargo reduces it, giving Shell insight into its market. The narrative $\pi _ { r } = { \left. { o u t b r e a k , r e s u p p l y } \right. }$ concludes before the embargo and keeps capacity at normal. It provides no information relevant to Shell’s decision, even though a war has erupted.

Definition 7 (Relevance). Given a topic T with planning problem $\Xi$ and concern fluents $C \subseteq V ,$ a narrative π is relevant if $\exists v \in C . \gamma ( I , \pi ) [ v ] \neq I [ v ] ( { \dot { \mathrm { i } } } . { \mathrm { e } } .$ , at least one concern fluent ends with a value other than its initial one).

The constraint $\varphi _ { \mathrm { r e l } } = \bigtriangledown \odot \bigvee _ { \boldsymbol { v } \in C } \boldsymbol { v } \neq \boldsymbol { I } [ \boldsymbol { v } ]$ implements Definition 7 in $\mathrm { L T L } _ { f }$ , where each $I [ v ]$ is a value read from the initial state. A formula □♢ψ is satisfied if and only if ψ holds at the final position. In Shell’s exercise, the constraint becomes □♢(capacity $\neq$ normal). The disruption narrative satisfies it because it ends with capacity at low, whereas $\pi _ { r }$ does not, because it ends with capacity at normal.

## 3.1.3 Challenge

Wilson [21] describes a scenario as challenging when it contests the organisation’s entrenched beliefs regarding the future. We judge whether a scenario is challenging by the values its final state gives the drivers D, which we call its out come: for drivers $D = \langle v _ { 1 } , \ldots , v _ { n } \rangle$ , the outcome of a narrative π is the tuple out $\mathbf { \Phi } _ { D } ( \pi ) = \langle \gamma ( I , \pi ) [ v _ { 1 } ] , \dots , \gamma ( I , \pi ) [ v _ { n } ] \rangle$ and we write $\mathrm { o u t } _ { D } ( \pi ) [ v _ { i } ]$ for its i-th value, as for every tuple over D. A narrative is challenging when its outcome differs from the topic’s conventional outcome $\widehat { D }$ (Definition 3). The conventional outcome is an input in its own right, and it coincides with the initial values of the drivers in Shell’s exercise only because Shell expected no change; an organisation that expected the cartel to split would state $\widehat { \cal D } = \langle f a l s e , f a l s e \rangle$ , and the engine would then treat a coordinated cartel as the challenging case. The disruption narrative $\pi _ { d } = \left. o u t b r e a k , r e s u p p l y , e m b a r g o \right.$ leads to an outcome where the cartel remains coordinated, but the region is at war $( \mathrm { i . e . , o u t } _ { D } ( \pi _ { d } ) = \langle t r u e , t r u e \rangle )$ ). Since this outcome differs from $\widehat { \boldsymbol { D } } = \langle t r u e , f a l s e \rangle$ on the war driver, the narrative is challenging.

## 3.1.4 Plausibility

Wilson [21] calls a scenario plausible if it can occur. We read this as two conditions on every narrative action: the action can be executed in its state, and it makes sense there. The first condition holds by default, since every narrative is $\mathtt { a } \equiv \mathtt { p l a n }$ . The second requires additional constraints, since a precondition lets the cartel impose an embargo but does not explain why. We borrow the two that narrative planning uses to make a story believable and the one that bounded planning uses to keep a plan loop-free. Narrative planning requires every character action to be intentional (i.e., a step of a plan towards one of the character’s goals) and every event to be causally connected to the rest of the story [31]. Sabre [32] reads intentions through a numeric utility per character, so that an action is intentional when it begins a plan that raises its actor’s utility. We adopt this approach, using actors’ objectives as utilities and the causal links of partial-order planning [45] as connections. We define both below.

Table 2 lists the three resulting conditions and their sources. Conditions are only as effective as the objectives the analyst defines. A condition will never trigger if the topic leaves out the input it depends on (for example, an intentional condition in a topic with no objectives).

Objectives and causal links. The conditions read the actors’ objectives O in T and the causal link. We define both before stating the conditions.

Objectives. Each actor g has an objective $u _ { q } : S \to \mathbb { R } \operatorname { ( i . e . }$ ., a function that assigns a number to each state), which is the utility that Sabre gives each character [32]. Sabre allows any numeric expression over the state. We restrict the objective to a weighted sum so a PDDL3 metric can express it. The analyst writes such objectives as PDDL3 preferences [27], each with a weight: a preference $\langle g , v , x , w \rangle \ { \stackrel { } { \in } } \ Q$ states that actor $\boldsymbol { g ^ { \prime } } \mathbf { s }$ objective gains $w > 0$ while the fluent $v \in V$ holds the value $x \in { \mathrm { r a n g e } } ( v )$ . For a numeric fluent, x is a direction, up or down, and the objective gains or loses w per unit of v. PDDL3 metric is-violated computes the objective of g at a state s as sum of the weights w of its preferences $\langle g , v , x , w \rangle$ with $s [ v ] = x , \operatorname { p l u s } \ w \cdot s [ \bar { v } ]$ for each numeric preference $\langle g , v , u p , w \rangle$ and minus $w \cdot s [ v ]$ for each $\langle g , v , d o w n , w \rangle$ . An action raises $\boldsymbol { g ^ { \prime } } \mathbf { s }$ objective when the state it reaches has a higher $u _ { g }$ than the state before it. For instance, Listing 1 gives the importer two preferences and the cartel one. The preference importer-resupplied is ⟨importer, resupplied, true, 1⟩: the importer’s objective gains 1 while resupplied is true. The term (capacity) of the importer’s metric is ⟨importer , capacity, up, 1⟩: the objective gains 1 per unit of capacity. The preference cartel-embargoed is ⟨cartel, embargoed, true, 1⟩. Along the disruption narrative, the importer’s objective is 1 in the initial state (the capacity), still 1 after the outbreak, 2 after the resupply, and 1 after the embargo, which lowers the capacity to 0 while resupplied stays true; the cartel’s objective is 0 until the embargo raises it to 1. So the resupply raises the importer’s objective and the embargo raises the cartel’s.

Causal links. An action that raises no objective can still matter because of what it prepares or what others did, the causal connectedness readers look for in a story [47]. A causal link [45] goes from an action to a later one when the first changes a fluent that the second requires as a precondition, and no action between them changes that fluent. A causal chain is a sequence of causal links, of any length within the budget. In Listing 1, the outbreak sets war, enabling resupply; resupply sets resupplied, enabling embargo. Thus, outbreak causally enables resupply, and resupply causally enables embargo, forming a causal chain among the three actions. Actions relate to their actors and targets in two key ways. An action replies to an earlier action when it is directed at the same actor as that earlier action. An action undoes a previous action by another actor when it changes a result back to a value that the earlier action set, and that value has remained the same since. In Shell’s exercise, no action replies to or undoes another. This is because no two actors direct actions at each other, and no action reverses another’s effect.

The conditions. We now state the three plausibility conditions $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , intentional, connected, and loop-free) and check each against the disruption narrative $\pi _ { d } = \langle o u t b r e a k$ , resupply, embargo⟩, which is plausible.

The intentional condition asks that an actor’s action serves its actor $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the action raises its actor’s objective, replies to an action aimed at its actor, or starts a causal chain that ends in an action that raises its actor’s objective or in a reply of its actor’s own). This can also be seen as setting up a motivated action. A chain may pass through other actors’ actions, since an actor may act so that another acts, and it may have any length within the budget. In $\pi _ { d } .$ , the resupply raises the importer’s objective, since it sets resupplied, which the importer wants, and the embargo raises the carte $\therefore \mathbf { s } ,$ since it sets embargoed, so both are intentional. The embargo lowers the importer’s capacity, which costs the importer, but the condition judges each action by its own actor’s objective. Note that, if the topic did not provide any cartel preference, the intentional condition would have rejected $\pi _ { d }$ because the embargo starts no chain and replies to nothing.

The connected condition requires the narrative to be one story. Every actor’s action after the first causally links to an earlier action, replies to one, or undoes one, so the narrative doesn’t split into unrelated threads. Every happening causally links to a later actor’s action. Thus, no event happens without consequence. In $\pi _ { d } .$ , the resupply is the first actor’s action, which the condition exempts; a causal link leads from the resupply to the embargo, since the embargo reads resupplied. A happening action leads from the outbreak to the resupply, since the resupply reads war, so both conditions hold.

The loop-free condition prevents an actor from acting from a state the narrative has already passed through, since intervening actions would lead nowhere. In $\pi _ { d }$ , no state repeats.

## 3.1.5 Differentiation and the Scenario Planning Problem

The previous parts covered the criteria for a single scenario. This part defines the criteria for a set of scenarios and the scenario planning problem. According to Cordova-Pozo and Rouwette [5], a differentiated scenario set must differ systematically on key variables, allowing analysts to trace each scenario to a different combination of driver values. Note that differentiation should not be confused with relevance, because relevance assesses the concern fluents of a single scenario, while differentiation compares the drivers across multiple scenarios. To clarify this, consider Shell’s exercise with the budget $c = 4$ and two scenarios, $\pi _ { 1 } ~ = ~ \langle o u t b r e a k$ , split, resupply, growth⟩ and $\pi _ { \mathcal { Q } } = \langle s p l i t$ , outbreak, resupply, growth⟩, together with the disruption narrative $\pi _ { d } = \langle o u t b r e a k$ , resupply, embargo⟩. The scenarios $\pi _ { 1 }$ and $\pi _ { \mathcal { Q } }$ differ only in the order of their events. In $\pi _ { 1 }$ , the war breaks out before the cartel splits, while in $\pi _ { \mathcal { Q } } ,$ the cartel splits before the war. Both scenarios leave a divided cartel in a war-torn region, whereas $\pi _ { d }$ ends with a coordinated cartel in the same region. Thus, two narratives that reach the same outcome by different routes, or that differ only on fluents outside $D _ { : }$ , count as the same. This makes the criterion checkable and separates it from SPA’s clustering (covered in Section 5) because the analyst’s drivers fix the grouping before planning rather than after.

Definition 10 (Differentiation). ## 3.2 Planning as Satisfiability

Planning as satisfiability [46] solves a planning problem by issuing a series of SAT queries $\mathcal { E } _ { 0 } , \mathcal { E } _ { 1 } , \mathcal { E } _ { 2 } , . . . ,$ , where each ${ \mathcal { E } } _ { n }$ checks whether a plan of length n exists. The encoding introduces a separate copy of every fluent at every time step, so the state s is given by the truth values of these copies at step i. It introduces a Boolean variable for each action at each step, indicating whether that action is executed at that time. The query is $\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { E } _ { n } : = \mathcal { T } ( s _ { 0 } ) \wedge \bigwedge _ { i = 0 } ^ { n - 1 } \mathcal { T } ( s _ { i } , s _ { i + 1 } ) \wedge \mathcal { G } ( s _ { n } ) } \end{array}$ Here, I specifies the initial state and G specifies the goal. The component T represents the transition function γ. Any model of ${ \mathcal { E } } _ { n }$ (i.e., any satisfying assignment) corresponds to a plan, obtained by reading off which actions are true at each step. The solver considers $n = 0 , 1 , 2 , . . .$ . until it finds a satisfiable query, so the first plan returned is of minimal length. In our setting, the trajectory constraints Φ of Section 3.1 replace the goal, and the budget c bounds a narrative’s cost. Every constraint $\theta \in \Phi$ is evaluated over the trajectory $\langle s _ { 0 } , \ldots , s _ { n } \rangle$ , and the last conjunct of the query is the budget constraint, which sums the costs of the actions that occur:

$$
\mathcal { E } _ { n } : = \mathbb { Z } ( s _ { 0 } ) \wedge \bigwedge _ { i = 0 } ^ { n - 1 } \mathcal { T } ( s _ { i } , s _ { i + 1 } ) \wedge \bigwedge _ { \theta \in \Phi } \theta ( s _ { 0 } , \dotsc , s _ { n } ) \wedge \left( \Big ( \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \sum _ { a \in A } \mathrm { c o s t } ( a ) \cdot \mathrm { o c c } _ { i } ( a ) \Big ) \leq c \right) ,
$$

An action atom a at step i is the variable $\operatorname { o c c } _ { i } ( a )$ , and a state atom $s [ v ] = x$ at step i is the copy of v at that step, where $\operatorname { o c c } _ { i } ( a )$ is 1 if the action a occurs at step i and 0 otherwise. A trajectory constraint becomes a formula over these variables by unrolling its temporal operators over the steps $0 , \ldots , n .$ . A step may hold no action, provided that every later step holds none either (i.e., $\textstyle \bigvee _ { a \in A } \operatorname { o c c } _ { i } ( a ) \to \bigvee _ { a \in A } \operatorname { \dot { o c c } } _ { i - 1 } ( a ) )$ , so each model of ${ \mathcal { E } } _ { n }$ corresponds to a scenario with at most n actions and a cost within the budget. We issue a single query with n large enough to fit every in-budget scenario in n steps, and the budget constraint bounds the cost. Enumerating the models of this query yields all in-budget scenarios.

The planner proposed by Abdelwahed [33] can solve this scenario-planning problem. Because FBI uses a feature model as formulas, a set of plans is diverse when the plans differ on the features [33]. It appends formulas to a planning-as-satisfiability query, so it can take our constraints directly, and a narrative’s outcome can serve as its behaviour. FBI finds a plan, forbids that plan’s behaviour, and calls the solver again, so it needs one call per outcome. We use ASP instead, because it gives the same result in one call. ASP is a declarative framework in which a problem is written as logic rules, and a solver returns answer sets (i.e., models of the rules, each of which is a solution). It first grounds the rules with the problem’s constants, and then solves the ground program. An ASP solver like clingo [48] enumerates and counts all answer sets of a program, which we use to generate all scenarios within the budget. With projection, it returns one answer set for each distinct assignment over a chosen subset of atoms [49]. Projecting on the outcome returns one scenario per outcome, as FBI would, without looping solver calls. The next subsection gives the implementation.

## 3.3 The ASP Implementation

An ASP program is a set of rules, and the solver’s task is to find its answer sets, sets of atoms the rules support. A rule has a head and a body, written head :- body, and it reads: the head holds whenever the body holds. The body is a list of atoms, each possibly under not (i.e., the atom is not derived), and the capitalised terms are variables that the solver instantiates with every object before solving (e.g., holds(V, X, t) stands for one atom per fluent V and value X). Two rule forms have special names: afact has no body, so its head always holds, and an integrity constraint has no head, so its body may never hold, removing any answer set in which it does.

We adopt the encoding of the planning problem of plasp [50], which converts a PDDL planning problem into ASP facts and solves it with an ASP planning encoding<sup>3</sup>, and we append to it one program per criterion of Section 3.1. The analyst writes the planning problem Ξ for a topic in PDDL3, which the plasp translates into the I and T encoding. For the scenario criteria, each is implemented in a logic program, and the programs are then appended to construct ${ \mathcal { E } } _ { n }$ . Each program realises its constraint as one or more laws, named patterns that no scenario may contain. A law ℓ is defined by the fact law(ℓ) and includes rules that derive broken(ℓ, t) whenever the pattern that violates the constraint appears at step t. All laws share one integrity constraint: the solver cannot produce a narrative that breaks any law. This means the solver eliminates such narratives during search rather than filtering them out later. Differentiation is a projection directive, which we cover later in this section. We will first explain the planning process, then outline the rules for each criterion in the order given in Section 3.1.

The planning encoding. plasp encodes ${ \mathcal { E } } _ { n }$ as ASP rules as follows (Listing 4). The atom holds(v, x, t) represents the copy of fluent v at step t with value x, and occurs(a, t) denotes the variable for action a at step t. The program is divided into three sections [48]. The base section captures I, and the step(t) section captures T from step t − 1 to t. The laws are split between step(t) and check(t). A law whose pattern is visible at its own step detects it in step(t), and a law that needs the whole narrative (the causal chains of the intentional law, relevance and challenge) detects it in check(t). The check(t) section holds the one constraint that enforces every law, grounded once at the last step, identified by the external atom query(t). For clarity, we use v, x, and a instead of plasp’s terms variable(v), value(variable(v), x), and action(a). The plasp implementation omits the initial value of a Boolean fluent that starts false unless an action reads it as false. The second rule of the base section ensures that every fluent without an initial value is false at step 0, as subsequent rules read these false values from the state. In the step(t) section, the choice rule ensures that at most one action occurs at each step. The subsequent integrity constraint (i.e., a headless rule that eliminates any answer set whose body holds) enforces the action’s precondition. The caused/3 rules implement the effects, and the following rule serves as the frame axiom. The encoding has exactly c steps, so a narrative with fewer than c actions leaves its last steps empty (i.e., with no action). The started/1 and ended/1 rules guarantee that a narrative begins at the first step and ends at its first empty step, so each such plan is generated exactly once. The last two rules of the step(t) section reject an actor’s action that changes no fluent, so a narrative never repeats an action to no effect. The final rule of Listing 4 is common to all laws and eliminates any answer set that breaks a law. In that rule, the atom waived(L) lets an analyst disable any law to suit their needs (i.e., adding the fact waived(ℓ) stops the law ℓ from removing any answer set), and the narratives the engine then accepts meet only the criteria whose laws remain.

## 4 Case Studies

In Section 3, we introduced the engine and applied it to Shell’s exercise. Here, we present a step-by-step guide showing how an analyst can reuse the engine for a new topic, and we illustrate the procedure for 2 topics from different domains. The procedure has 4 steps, and each writes a part of the topic $\mathbb { T } = \langle \Xi , \Gamma , \mathcal { O } , C , D , \widehat { D } \rangle$ : the analyst develops the planning domain (Step 1) and poses the question that turns it into a topic (Step 2), a script grounds the initial state (Step 3), and the engine generates the set (Step 4). Each topic is then covered in its own subsection, with 1 paragraph per step.

Step 1: develop the planning domain. The analyst writes the planning domain, denoted $\Xi ,$ in PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language). This includes specifying its objects, its fluents (Boolean or numeric), and action schemas that outline preconditions and effects. The first parameter of a schema names its actor. When the action targets an object, the second names it. A happening names no actor. The domain describes what can occur within the subject matter rather than detailing what the organisation inquires about it. Therefore, 1 domain can address all questions related to the same subject. When the subject has an event taxonomy, each schema can represent a specific event type. If no taxonomy exists, the analyst compiles schemas from existing literature. The following 2 subsections provide 1 example of each approach. This step is conducted manually, and the engine evaluates narratives based solely on the topic. Consequently, scenarios cannot include events outside the domain.

Step 2: pose the question and write the topic. Scenario planning begins with what an organisation needs to know. Schoemaker [2] starts by defining the analysis scope: name the organisation and the decision it faces, then state its concerns (what must hold at the end of a scenario) and its drivers (uncertainties whose outcomes distinguish scenarios). The question turns the domain into a topic. It decides which domain fluents are concerns $C$ and which are drivers D, and it names the organisation from whose side the criteria read a narrative. It also sets the actors’ objectives O (i.e., their weighted preferences) and the topic’s invariants (trajectory constraints in Φ). Every element in the topic translates directly into facts and rules, except Ξ, which translates into plasp rules. In Shell’s exercise, the concern was the importer’s ability to buy oil, and the drivers were cartel coordination and regional war. After this step, the record includes every element of T except the initial state of Ξ and the conventional outcome ${ \widehat { D } } ,$ which the record supplies.

Step 3: ground the initial state. A script compiles a board $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the planning problem for the topic, with an initial state read from the record, a list of objects, and a date). The drivers’ values on the board yield the conventional outcome ${ \widehat { D } } ,$ completing the topic. 1 script serves any combination of objects and dates, although a board is only as accurate as the thresholds the analyst sets for reading the record.

The planning domain (Step 1). The planning domain includes 6 countries and 65 action schemas. Each schema is tagged with the CAMEO root it abstracts (one of 20 top-level event types), and every root has at least 1 schema, spanning public statements (01), consulting (04), threatening (13), coercing (17), and fighting (19). Fluents place each country pair on a 4-rung ladder (neutral, tense, hostile, in-conflict), record ties (partnership, alliance, talks, border, dependence, aid, sanctions, massed forces, occupation), track population discontent, and assign each country a numeric economy. Most actions shift a pair 1 rung or change a tie: threats raise tense to hostile; strikes/attacks raise hostile to conflict; ceasefires reverse this; de-escalation requires talks. Threats depend on economies, and each country chooses its own events. The preconditions explain what makes an action believable. For example, an action with a high intensity of 4 needs either 2 sides that are already at war or forces focused on the target. A covert operation requires either a hostile pair or a pair that is at war. Forming an alliance or a new partnership needs states that are either in discussions or already partners.

The question and topic (Step 2). An analyst poses a question for an organisation trading across 6 countries: which wars between them are possible, and how would each affect trade? It focuses on 15 trade partnerships and 6 economies. A scenario matters only if a partnership forms or breaks, or if an economy ends differently than it began. The drivers are whether each of the 15 possible country pairs is at war. This setup treats a settlement and a frozen stalemate as the same outcome and includes 1 scenario for every war combination within the budget, so the set grows quickly with the number of pairs. The concerns are C = {partners, economy} for each pair and country, and the drivers D are in-conflict(s, t) for each country pair s, t. Countries are the actors; the first argument of each schema names the acting country, and the second the country it is directed at. Their objectives come from motives read from the record and a maintained table (i.e., each a preference with the motive’s weight). Each schema has an intensity 1–4: 4 war, 3 skirmish/covert operation, 2 sanctions/threats/demands/mobilisation/arming, and 1 renouncing an alliance or a diversionary dispute. Schemas are coercive from intensity 2. A tie holds between 2 countries when any standing relation links them. This topic adds its own laws. The escalation law allows the highest-intensity action only after earlier coercion between the same pair (either direction). The stranger law allows coercion only between countries that share a tie or have interacted before. The reversal law forbids a country from undoing the pair’s last shift if the other has not directed any action at it since. In Listing 10, directed(C, S, T, t) means the step-t action has schema C, actor S, and target T, and coerciveBeat(S, T, t) means that action is coercive. Without intensities and ties, these laws cannot fire, so the engine would allow a sudden attack between unrelated countries with no prior pressure. The capitulation law permits a concession (that is, granting a demand, giving in to pressure, or yielding under duress) only if the conceding state has previously been pressured by the other party (i.e., the other coerced it at intensity 2 or higher earlier in the narrative, imposed sanctions on it, had forces massed against it, or was hostile toward it or at war with it at the outset).

## 4.2 Supply Chains

A shipping-route disruption delays parts that manufacturers source internationally, affecting container lines, suppliers, and factories. This analysis focuses on 4 key routes: the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal, the port of Baltimore, and the Strait of Hormuz, and covers incidents from 1 January 2021 to 1 January 2026. We use action schemas from supply-chain risk literature, rather than an event taxonomy, based on pre-2021 sources.

The planning domain (Step 1). The planning domain includes 17 action schemas covering container lines, the maker (the manufacturer whose question the board answers), rival buyers, their supplier, and route-keeping authorities, as well as 6 happenings. The happenings are 5 risk categories from Chopra and Sodhi [53] (accident blockage, ship attack, drought/low water, demand surge, late delivery) plus closure by force, following Talmadge [54] on Iran and the Strait of Hormuz (a wide strait cannot be closed by a single accident). Buyer moves follow Tomlin [55]: use stock, fly parts, shift orders to a second source, or accept the loss and stop a line. Buyers can inflate orders (the rationing game of Lee et al. [56]); suppliers can ration and announce output. Lines can reroute, cancel sailings, or add a surcharge, following Notteboom [57]. Authorities can clear blockages (recovery in Sheffi and Rice [58]) or escort ships, as in Operation Atalanta since 2008.<sup>8</sup> Fluents track whether a route is closed or under attack, whether lines have diverted, and whether a buyer is short of parts, has inflated its order, or has stopped a line. 4 numeric fluents range from 0 to 3: route capacity, lane lateness, freight rates, and buyer stock. Most actions shift 1 level: blockage or forced closure shuts the route and makes the lane 2 levels late; a late delivery leaves the buyer short; drawing on stock covers the shortage but reduces stock by 1 level. This cost is coarse-grained, since a late delivery counts as 1 event regardless of duration.

The question and topic (Step 2). The analyst asks how a disruption could affect the parts stock of a European manufacturer whose parts ship from Asia via the Suez Canal. Because the concern is stock, a scenario matters only if the manufacturer’s stock ends at a different level than it began. The concern C is the maker’s stock. The drivers are (1) whether the canal is still closed at the end, (2) whether container lines divert, and (3) whether the manufacturer halts a production line. We chose these because post-event traffic data identify the first 2, and a dated announcement identifies the third. Lines want ships moving and rates up; buyers want stock up, no missed deliveries, and lines operating. Suppliers want only fillable orders; authorities want open, safe routes. ## 5 Related Work

In Section 2.1, we noted that an analyst cannot write out every trajectory of a topic by hand, and Sections 3 and 4 presented an engine that does so and applied it to two topics. In this section, we relate the engine to earlier efforts to automate scenario planning. These efforts fall into four categories: assembling driver states based on consistency, speeding up the modelling tasks involved in the manual process, producing scenarios using planning technologies, and creating scenario drafts with generative models.

Consistency-based combination. The earliest strand focuses on automating end-state selection. Weimer-Jehle [13] present cross-impact balance analysis, which evaluates every combination of driver states using pairwise influences elicited from analysts and retains only combinations with internally consistent scores. Ritchey [14] maps the same combinatorial space through morphological analysis and reduces it using pairwise consistency assessments. Bradfield et al. [59] review cross-impact analysis, from which cross-impact balances descend, and the intuitive-logics approach initiated by Shell, treating them as distinct schools within the same broader practice. In both approaches, a consistency score specifies what qualifies a particular combination of driver states as a scenario. Our approach applies consistency constraints to every narrative state, and a combination of driver states counts as an outcome only when a plan reaches it within budget.

Accelerating the manual exercise. The second group keeps analysts in charge while automating model construction. Davis et al. [6] reviews the methods proposed for automating the construction of simulation scenarios. Davis [7] speeds up scenario development by using natural language processing to extract a fuzzy cognitive map from documents, enabling the simulation of quantitative scenarios based on that map. These approaches depict the world as a network of interconnected drivers, so a generated scenario illustrates how various trends can reinforce or dampen one another. Our scenarios are instead sequences of events that actors cause, together with the states these events lead to. We keep analysts in control of the model (i.e., they provide the actions, constraints, and drivers), and we automate narrative generation, although our finite value ranges can only approximate the gradual changes a cognitive map expresses directly.

Planning-based generation. The closest system to ours is the IBM Scenario Planning Advisor (SPA) [9, 10, 11]. Sohrabi et al. [9] develop a model of causal forces by gathering analyst insights and then compiling them into a planning problem. In this model, action costs are derived from the elicited likelihoods, so a cheaper plan represents a more likely explanation of the observed risk drivers. They produce a set of high-quality plans using top-k planning [12, 60], then cluster them into scenarios [9, 11]. Our approach differs from SPA in three ways. First, while SPA ranks plans by likelihood, we assess plausibility without assigning costs or probabilities. Second, SPA’s plans explain observed risk drivers, whereas ours start from a stated present and operate within a set budget. Third, SPA groups similar plans after planning, while our criteria are integral to the planning process and guided by the analyst’s drivers. Within a similar domain, Smith et al. [61] create training scenarios using narrative planning. Their goal is skill acquisition rather than exploring alternative futures. Political-military wargaming produces adjudicated event sequences that more closely resemble our scenarios than driver-based scenarios do [62]. In this context, a human adjudicator determines which moves are permissible, similar to the preconditions and constraints of our planning problem. We borrow narrative planning’s conditions for a believable story, intentional characters, and causally connected events [31], along with Sabre’s reading of intentions through character-level utility [32]. However, we differ in what we search for, which is narrative planning aims for a specific outcome set by the author, while we explore possible futures without a defined goal.

Generation with large language models. The last group drafts scenarios directly with large language models (LLMs). For instance, Ferrer i Picó et al. [15] incorporate generative tools into a participatory foresight exercise to create future scenarios, while LLMScenario uses a language model to generate driving scenarios [16]. In our approach, a narrative is an executable plan, and a scenario is a narrative that meets the criteria; neither is something a language model can guarantee, nor does either read as fluent prose, and both require a planning model of the topic.

Among these four categories, only planning-based systems return a sequence of events, and none state a criterion for when the whole sequence counts as a scenario. The scenario planning problem in Definition 11 provides that criterion, and the Section 3 engine solves it in one solver call.

## 6 Discussion, Conclusion & Future Work

Discussion. In this paper, we frame scenario planning as a diversity planning problem and introduce an engine that generates one scenario for each potential outcome of a topic within a specified budget. The approach gives the analyst three key benefits. First, it presents every scenario as a narrative. This lets the analyst understand how a future unfolds, not just where it ends. Second, it applies the criteria uniformly as hard constraints across all narratives, with no ranking, so familiar futures do not get an advantage over unfamiliar ones. Third, the analyst can rerun it whenever conditions change, since a script rebuilds the board from the day’s sources, and the same topic then returns a new set. The budget bounds what any run can return, since each outcome needs a chain of events that fits within it. A narrative within a given budget also fits within any larger budget, so the set can only expand as the budget increases. For instance, the conflict set grows from 5 outcomes at budget 4 to 8 at budget 5, 9 at budget 6 and 11 at budget 7 (Table 5). The budget-7 set adds 3 outcomes to the 8 in budget 5, each holding two wars that five events could not, including the Russia–Ukraine war and the Iran–US war, which were underway together in June 2025. Therefore, a small budget misses outcomes that require longer chains, whereas a larger one reaches more of them at the cost of time and memory (Table 5).

The engine can return only what the topic models: it searches the narratives the model allows, so an event that is not modelled never appears in a scenario, however often it occurs in reality. For example, because the supply-chain domain lacks a rail land bridge, the Panama set cannot represent the 2024 diversion. Constructing a topic is also much easier when a taxonomy exists. The conflict domain adheres to CAMEO, allowing each of its 65 schemas to represent one event type, while the same taxonomy organises the news that informs its board. In contrast, the supply-chain domain lacks such a taxonomy, so we assembled its 17 schemas from five different sources and determined their precondition and effects ourselves. A topic built this way records the analyst’s every choice, and the set inherits those choices.

Conclusion. In this paper, we show that scenario planning can be framed as a diversity planning problem, in which two scenarios differ when they end in different outcomes on the analyst’s drivers. Polyscene encodes scenario criteria as trajectory constraints and returns a set containing one scenario for each possible outcome the topic can achieve within a given budget. In return, the analyst must model the topic accurately, and a small budget narrows the set to the outcomes that short chains can reach. We applied Polyscene to geopolitical conflicts and shipping-route disruptions as of 1 January 2021. With a 5 budget, the conflict set puts five of the 15 pairs at war. By 1 January 2026, three of those wars occurred (Russia–Ukraine, Iran–Israel, Iran–U.S.), and each ends a scenario. None of the ten pairs left at peace at this budget fought, and neither of the other two pairs marked at war did. On the supply-chain boards, the sets hold the Red Sea diversion, the closure and diversion at Baltimore, and the partial diversion at Panama.

Future work. Two directions remain. First, prune reorderings of independent events to reduce time and memory costs for larger budgets. Many narratives differ only in the order of independent events, so the solver explores multiple orders that yield the same outcome. For instance, in the fifth row of Table 6, Iran and the United States mobilise; because the mobilisations read and write different fluents, either can occur first and both yield the same result. Differentiation keeps one narrative per outcome, so the engine returns only one, but the solver still searches all orders, which grow factorially with the number of independent events. Second, let the analyst choose the narrative representing an outcome. Let a topic leave some initial unknowns when the record provides no information about them. Currently, the script sets a specific value for each unknown, and each scenario uses that fixed value. In assumption-based planning [63], a planner can choose a value for an unknown and include this assumption in the plan. A scenario could then depend on this stated assumption rather than a value the script decided. This approach could lead to a wider range of possible outcomes based on that assumption, but it requires the reader to check both the assumptions and the record for accuracy.
